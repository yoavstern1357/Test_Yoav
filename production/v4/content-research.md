## How to read this file

Confidence tags used on every claim:

| Tag | Meaning | On-screen wording |
|---|---|---|
| **FIRM** | Documented, archaeological, or stated by an official/government/major-museum source | Say it plainly |
| **LEGEND** | A traditional story that nobody has proven | "Legend says..." |
| **DISPUTED** | Reliable sources disagree (date, place, number) | "Many people believe..." or avoid the number |
| **FOLK** | A popular custom or explanation (etymology, lucky meaning) that is widely repeated but not scholarly | "Many people say..." |
| **WEAK** | One source, a travel blog, or I could only see a search excerpt | Verify at the source before it goes on screen |

Method note and honest limit: the page-fetch tool could not open several key hosts from this environment (Wikipedia, SCMP, english.shanghai.gov.cn, theworldofchinese.com were all blocked at the network level). Most facts below therefore come from search-result excerpts of those pages plus pages that did open through search. Where a claim rests on an excerpt only, I mark it **WEAK** or note "verify". Re-open the cited URL once before a fact goes on screen. All URLs are ones that came back in search results.

Brand rule: restaurant and chain names appear in this file for context only. Per CLAUDE.md and the season map, do not name shops or chains on screen. Describe them ("a famous Taipei chain", "a shop that is over 100 years old").

## Cross-episode: two moods and comedy-safe lines

### Mood A: "ancient Silk Road" (what the research gives us)
The three episodes line up as a real history ladder, oldest first:

1. About 2,500 years ago: the oldest physical "dumpling-like" food found so far (Shandong, a bronze container in a tomb). [FIRM that it was found, DISPUTED that it is a dumpling]
2. 2nd century BC: Guangzhou's Han-era king's tomb holds African ivory, frankincense and a silver box in a Persian style. [FIRM that the objects exist, DISPUTED where the silver box was made]
3. Six Dynasties to Tang (about 220-907): real dumplings from dry desert tombs at Astana, Turpan, on the northern Silk Road. [FIRM]
4. 9th century: an Arab sailing ship wrecked off Indonesia with about 60,000 Chinese ceramics. [FIRM]
5. 10th century: the twin brick pagodas of Nanxiang were built. [FIRM, roughly]
6. 13th century: Beijing becomes the Mongol capital Khanbaliq, the eastern end of the steppe road in Marco Polo's time. [FIRM that the city existed; "terminus" is a popular label]
7. Late 1800s: the soup dumpling story. [LEGEND, DISPUTED]

This is a ready-made "Dad's journal gets older as we go" idea: Ep 1 is the newest (1800s), Ep 2 is the sea road (2,000 years), Ep 3 reaches into the desert tombs.

### Mood B: "tour-guide host" (what the research gives us)
Every episode has easy "stand here and say this to camera" landmarks with a sound and a smell: Nanxiang old street (steam, bamboo, black vinegar), Guangzhou morning-tea hall (cart clatter, Cantonese chatter, tea), Beijing hutong in winter (couplets, pigeon whistles, stacked cabbages). Details are in each episode section.

### Comedy-safe rules (jokes land on the Uncle, never on a place or people)
- Safe targets: Uncle's appetite, speed, strength, impatience, bad folding, over-confidence, failing a custom the locals do gracefully.
- Never joke about: the "eat everything" stereotype of Cantonese food (see Ep 2), Chinese accents or language, politics, Xinjiang or Hong Kong/Taiwan status, religion (the Guangzhou mosque, the Kitchen God, offerings), who "owns" a dumpling, emperors as villains.
- Rival-city claims (Shanghai vs Changzhou vs Kaifeng; north vs south) are friendly pride. Present as "everyone wants to claim it", never "who is right".
- Chain names: keep them off screen. A "famous Taipei chain" is safe.
- Say "Spring Festival" (as the scripts do), not only "Chinese New Year": many countries celebrate the Lunar New Year.

## 1.2 Ancient trade and Silk Road links (what is real, what is a stretch)

- **Shanghai's older port was not Shanghai.** Qinglong Town (west of today's centre) was a major Tang and Song trading port. Excavated ceramics from it also turn up in Japan and Korea (the old kingdom of Silla), and archaeologists say it may be the earliest overseas-trade port in the Shanghai area and an important starting point of the Maritime Silk Road. [FIRM archaeology, "may be the earliest" is interpretation]. Founding date 746 or "fifth year of Tianbao" (Tang). https://archive.shine.cn/metro/Ancient-relics-of-Qinglong-may-rewrite-history-of-city/shdaily.shtml · https://archaeology.org/news/2016/12/08/161208-shanghai-silk-road
- **Shanghai's own timeline:** Huating (Tang, county founded 751) -> Shanghai becomes a market town in 1074 (Northern Song) -> county status 1291 (Yuan). [FIRM, with minor date variation between sources] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Shanghai
- **Nanxiang's ancient layer:** a temple founded in the Liang dynasty (505 AD, traditional date) gave the town its name; the town was a "prosperous trading hub" by the Tang. [FIRM that the Liang-era temple is the most consistent claim; the exact name varies: Baihe Nanxiang Temple / Yunxiang Temple]. https://english.shanghai.gov.cn/en-ScenicSpots/20240318/d27e0a0fd8a647f38e8da351b13cfeec.html · https://archive.shine.cn/district/jiading/A-brief-history-of-Nanxiang-Temple/shdaily.shtml
- **How the dumpling "travelled":** Kaifeng soup bun -> (maybe) Jiangnan towns (Changzhou/Wuxi) -> Nanxiang -> Shanghai city (about 1900) -> Taipei (a Shanghai-trained chef helped a Taipei shop develop its soup-dumpling recipe; that shop went from oil seller to dumplings in 1972) -> the world. The Taipei step is **FIRM**; the earlier steps are **DISPUTED**. https://michiganpublic.org/2023-03-29/yang-bing-yi-patriarch-of-taiwans-soup-dumpling-empire-has-died
- **Honest limit:** nothing ties the xiaolongbao itself to the Silk Road. The truthful Silk Road bridge is the *place* (Qinglong port) and the *wheat-dumpling family* it belongs to (see Ep 3). Do not claim "xiaolongbao came by caravan".

## 1.3 Surprising, funny, "wait, really?" facts

1. **The soup is a jelly when it goes in.** Pork-skin (and bone) stock is boiled for hours (a Nanxiang chef account says about four hours), chilled until it sets, then chopped into the meat. Steam melts it back into soup *inside* a skin that does not leak. [FIRM] https://www.goldthread2.com/food/shanghai-nanxiang-soup-dumpling/article/3125204 · https://www.thedailymeal.com/eat/here-s-how-they-put-soup-inside-soup-dumplings
2. **A famous Taipei chain weighs every dumpling: exactly 21 g (about 5 g of dough and 16 g of filling) with 18 folds.** The chain says 18 gives the soup room to expand; others say 18 is lucky. Other cooks use 16 folds; one source mentions 32 for a rival shop. [FIRM that the chain publishes 18/21 g; the 5/16 split is **WEAK**]. https://sg.style.yahoo.com/18-folds-21-grams-soup-110035257.html · https://dtf.com/us/about-us.html
3. **The "official" Nanxiang fold count is disputed** (14, 16 and 18 all appear). A good use of that: the Boy says "somewhere between fourteen and eighteen, depending who you ask", Uncle counts one. [DISPUTED] https://www.goldthread2.com/food/shanghai-nanxiang-soup-dumpling/article/3125204
4. **A Nanxiang head chef's shop makes over 2,000 soup dumplings a day**, and every table is within a 30-second reach of the kitchen (the reporter's detail). [WEAK, single article] https://www.goldthread2.com/food/shanghai-nanxiang-soup-dumpling/article/3125204
5. **The famous bun did not start small.** Its first name was "big meat bun". The thing that made it famous was getting smaller and juicier. (Good for a tour-guide line: "It became a star by shrinking.") [LEGEND]
6. **A bun that is a joke played on a river god.** The word "mantou" (bun) has a folk story attached: the strategist Zhuge Liang, crossing a river in the south, was told the river god needed human heads. He used dough "heads" filled with meat instead. The pun on "barbarian head" (mán tóu) is said to become "mantou". First written down in a Song-dynasty book. [LEGEND, and the pun is **FOLK** etymology; the "barbarian" wording is a sensitivity, see 1.7] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantou · https://greatbritishchefs.com/features/bao-steamed-buns-history · https://shine.cn/feature/taste/2103176085
7. **Noise is allowed.** One travel writer notes that in Shanghai it is acceptable to sip the soup noisily. [WEAK] https://www.scmp.com/lifestyle/food-drink/article/2099549/how-eat-xiaolongbao-foolproof-guide-eating-soup-dumplings
8. **Different cities, different sweetness.** The recurring claim: Changzhou's is fresher, Shanghai's sweeter, Wuxi's sweetest. [FOLK/DISPUTED] https://www.theworldofchinese.com/2017/11/steam-of-rivals/
9. **Gelatin bonus for the curious:** wheat-skin dumplings can be steamed but not boiled, because the thin wrapper would burst. [FIRM, recipe science] https://www.americastestkitchen.com/lessons/articles/2371-dumpling-science-and-how-to-make-dumplings-juicy
10. **Records:** no verified Guinness record for the largest xiaolongbao turned up. A Philadelphia restaurant advertises "America's largest soup dumpling" (a local claim). Do not use a record claim. https://abc7.com/videoClip/16196877/ (local claim only)

## 1.5 Food craft and eating properly (safety-relevant)

**How it is made (pro details)**
- Skin: unfermented flour dough (Nanxiang style), rolled so the centre is thick enough to hold the filling and the edge is thin enough to pleat. Chefs use a small rolling pin; some use a double-pin method. [FIRM / WEAK mix] https://www.goldthread2.com/food/shanghai-nanxiang-soup-dumpling/article/3125204
- Filling: ground pork plus the jelly; ginger, scallion, soy; sometimes crab roe in modern versions. Jelly pieces small so the soup is spread evenly. https://www.tastingtable.com/1734422/soup-dumplings-gelatin-ingredient
- Folding: the pleats spiral up to a twisted knot at the top. Too thin a fold tears, too thick leaves raw dough. https://www.goldthread2.com/food/shanghai-nanxiang-soup-dumpling/article/3125204
- Steaming: bamboo baskets, never boiling. https://www.americastestkitchen.com/lessons/articles/2371-dumpling-science-and-how-to-make-dumplings-juicy

**How to eat it (matches the script's "lift, spoon, tiny hole, wait, sip")**
1. Lift gently by the top knot with chopsticks (do not tear the bottom) and put it on a soup spoon.
2. Make a small opening (bite or poke) to let steam out, sip the soup first. Soup is hot enough to scald.
3. Eat the rest with black vinegar (often Zhenjiang vinegar) and thin ginger strips.
4. Do not eat one whole in one go. This is already the Uncle's gag. [FIRM, consistent across sources]
https://www.scmp.com/lifestyle/food-drink/article/2099549/how-eat-xiaolongbao-foolproof-guide-eating-soup-dumplings · https://thetakeout.com/how-to-eat-xiao-long-bao-soup-dumplings-1827959964 · https://www.mashed.com/1456995/why-vinegar-pairs-dipping-sauce-soup-dumplings/ · https://nowboarding.changiairport.com/discover-changi/why-you-should-not-pop-that-xiao-long-bao.html

**Common mistakes (good Uncle material)**
- Stabbing it so the soup runs out onto the plate; dropping it in vinegar before sipping; piling dumplings on top of each other so the bottoms tear on the basket.
- Eating it whole (steam burn). Waiting too long so the jelly-soup cools and the skin sticks.

## 1.7 Sensitivities (Ep 1)
- **Origin pride:** Nanxiang, Changzhou, Wuxi, Kaifeng all have claims. Use "many cities want to claim it", never "X did not invent it".
- **"Mantou = barbarian head"** story carries a historical slur-like term for southern peoples. If used, say only "a general fooled a river with dough heads" and skip the pun.
- **Brand:** do not show or name the Taipei chain. The "21 g / 18 folds" fact is fine as "a famous chain in Taipei".
- **Shops in Nanxiang:** two named old shops compete for "most authentic". Show a generic old shop, no sign, as the existing script does.
- **Taiwan:** mentioning a Taipei chain is fine as geography; do not add commentary about politics.
- **Heat/burns:** the Uncle's swallow-whole gag is already followed by the right-way beat. Keep it. Do not show a child copying him.

## 1.8 Top 10 hooks (Ep 1)
1. How do you put soup inside a dumpling? You make the soup solid first.
2. The soup dumpling started out as a "big meat bun", and it only became a star by getting smaller.
3. Nobody can agree who invented it: a Shanghai town, a Jiangsu teahouse, a Song-dynasty capital and an emperor on a boat all want the credit.
4. One famous chain weighs every dumpling at exactly 21 grams and counts 18 folds. Another shop says 16. Who is right? (Uncle counts one.)
5. The town's two brick towers are about 1,000 years old, nearly nine centuries older than the dumpling, and they survived the fire that burned the temple.
6. Shanghai's first overseas port wasn't Shanghai: ships left Qinglong with ceramics bound for Japan and Korea.
7. The soup is made by boiling pork skin for hours, then chilling it until it is solid enough to cut.
8. A bun with a river-god story: a general once fooled a river with dough "heads" (legend), which is said to be how the word "mantou" began.
9. In Shanghai it is fine to sip the soup loudly, and the right order is soup first, bun second.
10. Steam, not boiling: this dumpling's skin is so delicate that a bubbling pot would burst it.

**How the dumpling travelled and changed:** har gow is a Cantonese invention whose wheat-starch skin is the hero. It followed Cantonese emigrants and dim sum culture to Hong Kong and then to teahouses worldwide. The Silk Road link is *place and trade culture*, not the dumpling itself.

## 2.3 Surprising, funny, "wait, really?" facts

1. **Guangzhou made yum cha a law in 2026.** A city passed rules so that morning tea and "fresh dim sum within 24 hours" are protected as heritage. [FIRM] (sources above)
2. **The skin has no gluten-forming protein.** Wheat starch is wheat with the gluten removed, so the skin is soft and see-through, not stretchy; it is mixed with boiling water and sometimes tapioca starch, and it is shaped by pressing, not rolling with a pin. [FIRM] https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/discover-the-delights-of-har-gow · https://www.foodnetwork.com/recipes/food-network-kitchen/har-gow-12470087
3. **Har gow steams in about 6-8 minutes** and is done when the skin turns clear. [FIRM] https://www.foodnetwork.com/recipes/food-network-kitchen/har-gow-12470087 · https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/15323-har-gow-crystal-shrimp-dumplings
4. **The chef's test.** Har gow is the dish that shows if the chef is good: thin skin that doesn't stick or tear, small enough to eat in one bite, at least 7 pleats. [FIRM] https://learning.hku.hk/ccch9051/group-8/items/show/2
5. **Tea code.** Tap two fingers to say thank you for tea; leave the teapot lid ajar for a refill. The tap is explained by a legend that Emperor Qianlong, travelling in disguise, poured tea for a servant who could not kneel without revealing him, so the servant bent two fingers instead (like a bowing person). Experts say it is probably not history. [LEGEND; the custom itself FIRM] https://www.tastingtable.com/1520166/two-finger-dim-sum-gratitude-etiquette · https://says.com/my/lifestyle/why-chinese-tap-table-tea-pour · https://www.tastingtable.com/1526807/how-to-get-dim-sum-water-refill
6. **"One cup, two items."** The classic way to order is one pot of tea and two dishes (yat jung leung gin, 一盅两件, in Cantonese). [FIRM as a cultural phrase] https://www.malaymail.com/news/eat-drink/2024/12/12/yum-cha-like-a-pro-in-guangzhou-at-these-three-essential-dim-sum-parlours/159607
7. **Morning tea hours:** teahouses open from about 4 am; morning tea runs to about 11 am; evening tea runs late. Locals treat it as a 1-2 hour social event. [FIRM, typical] https://www.gz.gov.cn/guangzhouinternational/visitors/whattodine/dietaryhabits · https://travelchinaguide.com/chinese-yum-cha.htm
8. **The city's nickname is the "City of Rams."** Legend: five immortals riding five rams arrived carrying rice ears and blessed the land with no famine. A giant Five Rams statue (about 11 m, built around 1959-1960) stands in Yuexiu Park. [LEGEND; the statue FIRM] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_the_Five_Immortals_(Guangzhou) · https://govt.chinadaily.com.cn/s/201612/23/WS5b77d41e498e855160e88392/five-ram-statue.html
9. **The tower's nickname is "Slim Waist"** (Xiao Man Yao) because of its twisting waist; it is about 600 m (604 m with antenna). [FIRM nickname; the "named after a Tang woman" story is **WEAK**] https://gz.gov.cn/guangzhouinternational/visitors/whattosee/designandarchitecture/content/post_2998358.html · https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_Tower
10. **Push carts are fading.** Many places now order by QR menu; a few old-style halls still roll carts. Some sources date pushcarts to the early 1960s. [WEAK] https://www.chinavigators.com/dim-sum-in-guangzhou/

## 2.5 Food craft and eating properly

**Pro details**
- Skin: wheat starch plus a little tapioca starch, hot water, rested. Press each ball thin (a flat blade or a tortilla-press-like tool), do not roll with a pin. https://www.surlatable.com/recipe/crystal-shrimp-dumplings-har-gow/REC-370276 · https://www.foodnetwork.com/recipes/food-network-kitchen/har-gow-12470087
- Filling: whole shrimp (salting makes them crunchier), bamboo shoot and sometimes pork fat; the shrimp "pink" shows through the skin. https://tasteasianfood.com/har-gow/
- Pleating: pinch the first pleat with the thumb and index finger, then push each next pleat with the other hand's index finger while turning, until round. https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/discover-the-delights-of-har-gow
- Mistakes (Uncle's): squeezing the skin (it tears), too dry or too wet dough, overfilling, steaming too long, using wonton skins as a substitute (they are not wheat starch). https://rasamalaysia.com/shrimp-dumplings-har-gow/comment-page-9

**How to eat it**
- Pick it up gently with chopsticks (hold the pleated top, not the sides), eat in one bite, with a little chili sauce or soy if wanted. It is eaten warm; steam from the basket is hot but, unlike a soup dumpling, there is no hot soup, so this is not a major burn hazard. Shrimp allergy line is needed on screen (shellfish).
- Tea first: pour for others before yourself (a common courtesy, **WEAK** source), and a host pours a cup about 70 percent full (a polite custom, **WEAK**). https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/how-to-eat-dim-sum-three-tips-from-yauatcha-waikiki/

## 3.2 Ancient trade and Silk Road links

- **The desert dumplings are the Silk Road link.** Turpan was an oasis on the northern Silk Road; the Astana cemetery served the ancient city of Gaochang (Western Jin to Tang). Dry air kept food, textiles, documents and dumplings. Present as "real food, on a real road". [FIRM] https://www.archaeologynow.org/blog/a-lucky-food-for-lunar-new-year · https://en.chinaculture.org/library/2008-01/15/content_39479.htm
- **The wheat-dumpling family went west.** Manti (a later Season 2 dumpling) is widely said to have travelled along the Silk Road across Central Asia to Anatolia with Turkic and Mongol peoples, with earliest manti-like mentions around the Mongol era (a 1330 manuscript); some researchers think the direction may have been east instead. Fair line for the Boy: "dumplings travelled the road, and nobody knows exactly who started it". [DISPUTED direction] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manti_(food) · https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/06/16/feature-dumplings-reveal-shared-silk-road-heritage-links-between-china-trkiye · https://www.euronews.com/2026/08/02/delicious-dumplings-the-evolution-of-delicacies-enjoyed-all-along-the-silk-road-to-spain
- **Beijing was the Mongol capital Dadu (Khanbaliq, "city of the Khan")** in the 13th century (construction from the 1260s; sources disagree on the exact years 1264/1266/1272 and completion 1293), and Marco Polo knew it under a name written "Taidu"/"Cambaluc" (spellings vary). A Silk Road course page calls it the eastern end of the grassland road; treat "terminus" as a popular label. After 1368 the Ming gave it a new status. This is a perfect bridge to Season 2's Marco Polo structure. [FIRM that Dadu existed; "terminus" is **FOLK**] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadu_(Beijing) · https://www.britannica.com/topic/Taidu
- **The word "hutong"** is widely thought to come from Mongolian (a word for "well", *quddug*) during the Yuan dynasty; a newer proposal suggests another Mongolian root. A fun, real "Mongol-era" link for the Beijing lane. [DISPUTED etymology, FIRM that it entered Chinese in Yuan times] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutong · https://ese-journals.unisalento.it/index.php/linguelinguaggi/article/view/32442
- **Transport today:** Beijing West to Guangzhou South is about 2,298 km on the world's longest high-speed rail line (opened December 2012); the fastest trains take about 7 hours 20 minutes to about 8 hours. The script's "about 2,300 km, about 8 hours" is right. [FIRM, timetables change] https://www.travelchinaguide.com/china-trains/beijing-guangzhou/ · https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/china-opens-world-s-longest-high-speed-rail-line-1.1190497

## 3.3 Surprising, funny, "wait, really?" facts

1. **The oldest dumplings found so far were in a tomb in a desert, and they are shaped like tiny crescent moons.** [FIRM] (sources 3.1)
2. **A doctor wrapped medicine in dough to save frozen ears.** A legend, but the dumpling really is shaped like an ear in some tellings. [LEGEND]
3. **The Qing emperors ate vegetarian dumplings on New Year's Eve** (an unwritten palace rule), while ordinary families ate meat after a lean year. [WEAK/FIRM-ish] https://news.cgtn.com/news/2022-01-28/Eating-dumplings-has-strict-rules-to-follow-since-Qing-Dynasty-17bzv3Z7ppm/index.html
4. **"Jiaozi" sounds like "the old year and new year crossing".** Dumplings are literally eaten at the moment the year changes. [FOLK] https://folklore.usc.edu/dont-break-the-dumplings-at-new-years-eve/
5. **A record-breaking migration is going home to fold them.** China's 2026 Spring Festival travel rush recorded about 9.41 billion inter-regional trips over 40 days, the peak day nearly 353 million trips, about 538 million of them by rail. [FIRM, official figures] https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-03-13/China-s-2026-Spring-Festival-travel-rush-sees-record-9-4-billion-trips-1LtVItyUhuU/index.html
6. **The TV Gala.** The national Spring Festival Gala started live in 1983 with very few TVs; families now watch while folding dumplings, with broadcaster claims up to about a billion viewers (unverified claims). [FIRM start, WEAK numbers] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMG_Spring_Festival_Gala · https://www.travelchinaguide.com/essential/holidays/new-year/gala.htm
7. **Beijing's pigeons wear whistles.** Light bamboo or gourd whistles are tied to pigeon tails; when the birds fly, the air makes a hooting chord. Listed as Beijing intangible cultural heritage (2014); the craft revived in recent decades. [FIRM] https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1061896256 · https://news.cgtn.com/news/2019-08-26/Chorus-of-Life-Pigeon-whistle-in-Beijing-JtyXYmU9y0/index.html
8. **Beijingers stock Chinese cabbage like a fortress every November**: tough, long-keeping, stacked and covered with quilts so it won't freeze, and eaten until about February. The habit has faded with apartments and supermarkets but not disappeared. Cabbage is also the soul of the dumpling filling. [FIRM, traditional] https://news.cgtn.com/news/79677a4e77637a6333566d54/index.html · https://www.thebeijinger.com/node/1390253 · https://www.theworldofchinese.com/2020/01/king-cabbage/
9. **The upside-down fu.** Hang "fu" (good fortune) upside down on the door: "upside-down" (dao) sounds like "arrive" (dao), so luck arrives. [FOLK; the pun is FIRM] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fu_(character)
10. **Sweeten the Kitchen God.** On "Little New Year" (the 23rd day of the last lunar month in the north, 24th in the south), families give the Kitchen God sticky maltose candy so he'll say sweet things (or be unable to talk) when he reports on the family to heaven. [FOLK] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitchen_God · https://www.theworldofchinese.com/2018/02/the-kitchen-gods-candy/
11. **A real traditional Beijing reunion dinner had twelve dishes (four cold, eight hot).** [FOLK/official tourism] https://english.beijing.gov.cn/beijinginfo/culture/beijingfolklore/festivals/202110/t20211018_2514753.html · https://english.visitbeijing.com.cn/article/47OqKNckLsk
12. **Dipping sauce is personal:** one Beijing family reported soy + garlic paste, black vinegar + chili oil, and a mix of vinegar, soy and garlic. [FOLK, single anecdote] https://gulfnews.com/food/north-to-south-chinese-community-in-the-uae-shares-the-new-year-table-customs-and-conversations-1.1674481049630
13. **Bonus (not Beijing):** a Guinness record for "most expensive commercially available dumpling" was set in 2018 by a restaurant in Munich at about 399 pounds. Use only as a wild aside, not tied to a country. [FIRM] https://guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/484351-most-expensive-jiaozi-gyoza-dim-sum-dumpling-commercially-available

## 3.5 Food craft and eating properly

**Pro techniques (good for the recipe montage)**
- **Stir in one direction.** Add liquid (water or stock) to ground pork gradually, stirring one way until it becomes a sticky paste. Add the cabbage last so it doesn't release water. [FIRM, widely taught] https://kitchenstories.com/en/recipes/chinese-jiaozi-pork-and-cabbage-dumplings · https://www.splendidtable.org/recipes/pot-stickers
- **Salt and squeeze the cabbage** for about 10-15 minutes, then squeeze very dry. [FIRM] https://redhousespice.com/pork-cabbage-dumpling/
- **Rest the dough** at least about 30 minutes. [FIRM] https://thedailymeal.com/recipes/jiaozi-dumplings-recipe-1/?mode=print
- **Fry, steam, then re-crisp:** brown the bottoms, add water and cover to steam, then take the lid off to crisp. [FIRM] https://redhousespice.com/cook-dumplings/ (already cited in the script)
- **Crispy "skirt" (extra drama):** replace the water with a thin starch slurry (an example: 1 tsp cornstarch + 1/2 tsp flour + 100 ml water for about 10 potstickers); as water evaporates a lacy crust forms. Stir right before pouring. Good animated shot. [FIRM, recipe blogs] https://redhousespice.com/chicken-potstickers-with-crispy-skirt/ · https://www.foodnetwork.com/recipes/food-network-kitchen/dumplings-with-a-crispy-skirt-11644222.amp
- **Use a nonstick pan, don't move them early, don't crowd, don't add too much water at once.** [FIRM] https://tastingtable.com/1503559/nonstick-pan-making-potstickers · https://tastingtable.com/1082957/how-hidden-air-pockets-could-ruin-your-homemade-potstickers

**How to eat it**
- Dip in black vinegar and soy, optionally with garlic or chili oil. Eat one at a time; rest a minute. The juices can be very hot. [FIRM]
- Serve crispy side up (the plate-flip move in the script). [FIRM]

**Common mistakes (Uncle fuel)**
- Squeezing the cabbage too hard (already a gag), overfilling so the dumpling bursts, skipping the water so the filling stays raw, lifting the lid early, moving the dumplings before they release (they tear), eating straight from the pan, and sealing a dumpling with air trapped inside. [FIRM-ish]

## 3.10 Script check (Ep 3)
- **Likely wrong (Beijing):** the final beat has "soft distant fireworks" through the window at midnight. Beijing has had a citywide fireworks ban since the 2021/22 season, reported as still being held. Swap for TV gala sound, lanterns, snow, a neighbour's laughter, or a "quiet night" (even better, a pigeon-whistle sound at dawn). https://english.beijing.gov.cn/latest/news/202112/t20211215_2561290.html · https://thebeijinger.com/node/1654226
- **Inconsistent quantity:** the montage and recipe use "1 heaped tsp filling" per wrapper, but 24 wrappers need about 16-20 g each (about 1 tablespoon) from the listed 250 g pork, 200 g cabbage and seasonings. A heaped teaspoon is only about 7-10 g and would leave about half the filling unused. The 24 count and filling weight do not match. Fix: "about 1 tablespoon filling". (The same issue was fixed in the earlier fact-check for the soup-dumpling recipe.) Computation shown in 3.11 below.
- **Fine, but modest:** "more than 1,000 years old" for the Astana dumplings is true and conservative; Tang ones are about 1,300-1,400 years old and some are older.
- **"Boil until they float":** floating is a common cue, not proof of doneness; the script also checks colour and temperature, which is right.
- **Midnight dumplings:** "many families in northern China" is the correct hedge.
- **Train:** "2,300 km, about 8 hours" matches (2,298 km; fastest runs about 7h20m-8h).
- **Correct:** the Zhang Zhongjing "legend says", the Astana "real history", guotie as "pot stick", ends-open shape, Beijing as a setting rather than a birthplace.

## 3.11 Filling-per-wrapper check (for the "heaped tsp" flag)
Pork 250 g + squeezed cabbage about 120-140 g (from 200 g raw) + scallion about 20 g + ginger, soy, oil, cornstarch, pepper about 25-30 g = roughly 420-450 g. Divided by 24 wrappers = about 17-19 g each, which is about 1 tablespoon. A heaped teaspoon is about 7-10 g, so only about half the filling would be used. (This is my own arithmetic from the script's quantities; the wrapper size and squeeze loss vary, so treat as approximate.)
